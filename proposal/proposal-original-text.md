Analyzing How Gaps in Industry -Standard Software Tools Affect the 
Entry Readiness of IS Graduates in Local Firms and Freelance Markets  
1. Background of the Study 
Undergraduate Information Systems programs aim to give students a balanced 
combination of technical capabilities, business process understanding, and 
problem-solving skills. According to guidelines from the Association for 
Information Systems (2020), the primary goal of IS education is to prepare 
graduates who can build, manage, and optimize technology solutions within 
modern digital organizations. However, over the past decad e, the software 
industry has undergone rapid transformation. Modern software development 
relies heavily on cloud environments, automated deployment pipelines, 
continuous integration tools, and collaborative version control platforms. As a 
result, software firms and clients expect entry-level IS graduates to possess both 
core conceptual knowledge and practical familiarity with industry -standard 
development tools. 
Despite these high expectations, many universities struggle to keep up with the 
fast pace of technological change in the software sector. Academic institutions 
face lengthy administrative approval and accreditation cycles, which often delay 
updating course syllabi (Radermacher et al., 2014). In many undergraduate IS 
curricula, teaching focuses heavily on fundamental concepts like data structures, 
system analysis, relational database design, and basic programming syntax. 
While these theoretical foundations are essential, students frequently graduate 
with minimal hands-on experience using the actual tool stacks that software 
teams rely on every day, such as Git/GitHub for version control, Docker for 
containerization, Jira or Trello for agile project tracking, and cloud platforms 
like AWS or Azure for application deployment. 
In our local technology market, this disconnect between academic instruction 
and industry practice presents serious challenges for new graduates. Local 
software firms operate in competitive environments where fast project delivery 
and code quality are critical. When entry-level graduates lack familiarity with 
modern software development workflows, companies are forced to spend extra 
time and financial resources on on-boarding and technical training. Stevens and 
Norman (2016) note that significant gaps in practical tool usage increase on-
boarding costs and lower early-career productivity. For local technology

companies, this creates friction during hiring and delays the time it takes for 
new employees to become fully productive team members.  
At the same time, a growing number of IS graduates enter the tech workforce 
through independent freelancing, contracting, or remote gig platforms. Unlike 
corporate employees, freelancers operate without structured company training 
programs or senior mentors to guide them. Freelancers are expected to ma nage 
their own code repositories, set up deployment pipelines, and use project 
management tools to collaborate directly with clients from day one. When 
freelance graduates lack proficiency in these industry-standard tools, they face 
immediate operational barriers that can jeopardize client satisfaction, project 
delivery, and income stability. Therefore, investigating how software tooling 
discrepancies impact graduate job readiness across both corporate and freelance 
settings is vital for improving IS higher education and supporting the local 
digital economy. 
2. Problem Statement 
Many undergraduate IS graduates achieve strong academic results in their 
coursework but still encounter significant difficulties when transitioning into 
professional software roles. A primary reason for this difficulty is the gap 
between the theoretical concepts taught in university and the hands -on software 
tools required by local employers and freelance clients. Studies by Garousi et al. 
(2019) and Lethbridge et al. (2007) highlight that while conceptual 
understanding of databases and programming is necessary, it is not sufficient on 
its own to guarantee workplace effectiveness. Graduates also need practical 
experience with industry-standard tools like Git, Docker, Jira, and cloud 
deployment platforms to participate effectively in modern software workflows.  
High academic achievement in theoretical IS courses does not automatically 
lead to high entry-level job readiness if students lack hands-on experience with 
production-grade development tools. Furthermore, this tooling gap impacts 
graduates differently depending on their employment pathway. In formal 
software firms, tool unfamiliarity leads to extended on-boarding periods, 
increased supervisory burdens on senior developers, and reduc ed initial 
productivity. In contrast, for independent freelancers who lack structured 
organizational support, tool unfamiliarity can result in failed project deliveries, 
damaged client relationships, and early career failure.

Currently, there is a lack of localized empirical research examining which 
specific categories of software tools create the biggest readiness barriers, or how 
these barriers differ between corporate developers and independent freelancers. 
Without clear data on these specific tool gaps, university faculty and curriculum 
committees lack the evidence needed to prioritize curriculum updates. This 
study addresses this gap by assessing the specific tool discrepancies between 
university IS instruction and local industry practices, and by evalu ating how 
these gaps affect graduate readiness in both firm and freelance settings.  
3. Research Questions 
To address the identified research problem, this study formulates the following 
two research questions: 
RQ1: Which specific categories of industry-standard software tools—such 
as version control systems, cloud and DevOps platforms, development 
frameworks, and agile project management software show the largest gap 
between undergraduate IS curriculum coverage and the operational 
requirements of local software companies and tech freelancers? 
RQ2: How do these identified software tooling gaps differentially affect the 
entry-level job readiness and early workplace performance of IS graduates 
employed in structured software firms compared with those operating 
independently in the freelance market? 
4. Research Objectives 
General Objective 
To examine the gaps between the software tools taught in undergraduate IS 
programs and those required in local software workplaces, and to evaluate how 
these tool gaps impact the entry-level job readiness of graduates across 
corporate software firms and freelance markets.  
Specific Objectives 
1. To identify and rank the specific categories of software tools where the 
gap between university IS curriculum coverage and local industry 
requirements is most severe. 
2. To analyze and compare how tool unfamiliarity affects graduate re adiness 
outcomes in corporate software firms (such as onboarding duration and

5. Proposed Research Method 
Research Approach: 
This study adopts a quantitative-dominant mixed-methods research design. A 
quantitative cross-sectional survey will serve as the primary data collection 
method to systematically measure tool exposure, industry tool requirements, and 
perceived job readiness across a broad sample. As explained by Creswell and 
Creswell (2018), integrating quantitative survey data with qualitative insights 
allows researchers to establish broad statistical patterns while capturing 
practical context. Therefore, short semi-structured follow-up interviews will 
supplement the survey data to provide deeper explanations of the specific 
operational challenges graduates experience. 
Data Source(s): 
Data will be gathered from two distinct target respondent groups within the 
local tech ecosystem. The first group consists of recent IS graduates (graduated 
within the past 1–3 years) and final-year undergraduate IS students who are 
actively preparing for or entering the job market. The second group comprises 
experienced industry practitioners, including senior software developers, tech 
team leads, IT hiring managers, and active tech freelancers operating in the 
local market. 
Data Collection Method(s): 
Primary data will be collected using an online structured questionnaire created 
on Google Forms and distributed through university alumni networks, local tech 
LinkedIn groups, and developer communities. The questionnaire will use 5 -
point Likert scales (ranging from 1 = 'No exposure/Not important' to 5 = 
'Extensive hands-on exposure/Critical importance') to collect standardized data 
on university tool coverage, industry tool requirements, and perceived 
readiness. Additionally, 4 to 6 semi-structured follow-up interviews will be 
conducted with tech leads and experienced freelancers to gather qualitative 
insights on specific workflow bottlenecks and onboarding challenges.

Data Analysis Method(s): 
Quantitative data collected from the survey will be exported and analyze d. 
Descriptive statistics and the Relative Importance Index (RII) will be calculated 
to identify and rank the software tool categories with the largest curriculum 
gaps to answer RQ1. To answer RQ2, independent samples tests will be 
conducted to compare readiness scores between firm-employed graduates and 
freelancers. Multiple regression analysis will also be applied to assess the 
statistical relationship between tool familiarity and overall readiness. Qualitative 
data from the interviews will be analyzed using thematic analysis to categorize 
key operational themes and challenges across both work settings.
